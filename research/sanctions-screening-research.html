Sanctions & entity data

Sanctions Research for Digital Risk Investigations

A screening hit is the beginning of the work, not the end of it. This note is about the part that follows: resolving whether the record and the subject are actually the same entity.

Published 2026-09-14 · Methodology note · Trustsniffer Security Research

Sanctions lists look like the one piece of unambiguous ground in digital risk work. A government publishes a list; a name is either on it or not. In practice the matching problem sits between the list and reality, and that problem is where nearly all the error lives.

1. What a sanctions list is

A sanctions list is an administrative record published by an authority — a national government, a supranational body, or a ministry acting under a specific legal instrument. Each entry identifies a person, organisation, vessel, aircraft or, increasingly, a cryptocurrency address, and attaches legal consequences within that authority's jurisdiction.

Three properties follow and are routinely forgotten:

  • A list is jurisdictional. A designation by one authority is not a global prohibition. Obligations depend on where a party operates, its nationality, its currency of settlement and often its ownership.
  • A list changes. Entries are added, amended and removed. A delisting is as operationally significant as a listing, and it propagates through downstream copies far more slowly.
  • A list is about identity, not behaviour. It tells you that an authority designated an entity. It does not itself describe what the entity did.

2. Entity resolution: the actual difficulty

Screening compares a subject against records that were written in a different context, often a different script, frequently decades ago. Deciding whether two descriptions denote the same entity is the discipline of entity resolution, and it is harder than it sounds.

Name collisions

Common names are common. A sanctions list containing a person with a widespread given name and family name will collide with a very large number of unrelated individuals. Sanctions records anticipate this by carrying discriminators — date of birth, place of birth, nationality, passport or national ID numbers, known addresses. A match on name alone, with none of the discriminators checked, is not a match. It is a candidate.

Transliteration and orthography

Names originally written in Arabic, Cyrillic, Persian, Chinese or Korean reach a Latin-script list through transliteration, and there is no single correct transliteration. One person can appear under several spellings across different authorities' lists, and a strict string comparison will miss all but one. Fuzzy matching solves that and creates the opposite problem: loosen the threshold enough to catch genuine variants and you begin matching genuinely different people. There is no threshold that avoids both, which is why the discriminator check is not optional.

Corporate identity

Organisations are harder than people. They restructure, rename, change jurisdiction, and operate through subsidiaries whose names resemble nothing on the list. Several regimes extend designation to entities owned or controlled above a threshold by a designated party, which means the relevant question is often about a shareholding structure that the list does not enumerate. That work requires corporate registry evidence, not a string comparison.

The discriminator rule

Escalate a name match only when at least one strong identifier agrees — date of birth, an identity-document number, a registration number, or a cryptographic address. Record which identifier matched. A hit you cannot describe in those terms is a hit you cannot defend.

3. Addresses on sanctions lists

Cryptocurrency addresses are a newer and, in one narrow respect, easier class of identifier: an address is an exact string, so matching is deterministic and transliteration does not arise. That precision creates its own traps.

  • The address is designated; adjacency is not. A designation covers the listed address. Whether an obligation extends to a counterparty that received funds from it is a legal question about the specific regime and the facts, not something the list answers.
  • Addresses outlive their listing context. Deposit addresses get reassigned, wallets change hands, and an address listed in connection with one operation may later be controlled by someone unconnected to it.
  • Chain matters. The same string can be valid on multiple EVM networks. A designation that names a network means that network.
  • Issuer freezes are not designations. A stablecoin issuer freezing a balance is a private contractual action, sometimes at law-enforcement request, sometimes not. Our own frozen stablecoin dataset separates the two into distinct columns because merging them produces false claims in both directions.

5. Verification, and why it cannot be skipped

Both error directions carry real cost. A false positive can freeze a legitimate customer's funds, terminate a legitimate business relationship and cause documented harm to someone who did nothing. A false negative can mean a genuine breach. Neither is a rounding error, and the asymmetry many teams assume — that over-flagging is the safe choice — is not true of the person wrongly flagged.

  • Open the entry at the publishing authority, not at an aggregator, and record the programme and the listing date.
  • Compare every available discriminator and write down which matched and which did not.
  • Establish that the entry is still current at the time you are relying on it.
  • Determine which regimes actually bind the party in question.
  • For an address, confirm the chain and the designation's stated scope.
  • Preserve the evidence: source URL, retrieval timestamp, and the record as it read that day.
  • Where the match remains ambiguous, say so explicitly rather than rounding to a verdict.

For an overview of publicly documented entities and addresses in a browsable form, our sanctions directory is a convenient starting index — though for anything with consequences, the authority's own publication remains the record that counts.

6. Writing it up

A sanctions finding should be readable by someone who will disagree with it. That means: the subject as you received it; the record matched; the authority, programme and listing date; the discriminators that agreed and those that did not; the confidence and what would change it; and the retrieval timestamp. Phrased that way, a reviewer can evaluate the reasoning instead of taking the conclusion on trust — which is the only form of finding that survives contact with a serious challenge.
